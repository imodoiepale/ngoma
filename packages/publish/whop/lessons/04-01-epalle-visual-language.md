# EPALLE visual language

A music video without a fixed visual language is a collection of nice shots. This lesson
defines the constraints that make separate shots read as one body of work — and constraints
are the point. Generative tools will happily give you infinite variety, and infinite variety
is what makes AI video look cheap.

## The palette

**Charcoal, ivory, dust gold.** Three values, held across every shot.

- Charcoal carries the frame — deep neutral, never pure black, so shadow keeps detail.
- Ivory is the light and the skin-adjacent tone — warm off-white, never clinical.
- Dust gold is the accent, and it is *dust*: matte, aged, sun-worn. Not shine, not metallic
  highlight, not the orange-teal reflex that generative models default to.

Write the palette into every prompt and check it on output. The most common drift is dust gold
warming into orange and charcoal crushing to black. Both flatten the image.

## Thematic vocabulary

The recurring material is **African visual poetry**: flowers, memory, burning, blooming,
rebirth. These are not decorations, they are the argument. A flower in this vocabulary is
doing time — it is either about to open or about to go — and that ambiguity is what carries
meaning across a cut.

The register is **fashion-film restraint**. That means:

- Composed stillness over motion for its own sake.
- One idea per shot.
- Silence and negative space allowed to sit.
- No visual effect that announces itself.

Restraint is also the practical choice: a still, well-composed generated shot holds up far
better than a busy one, because artefacts hide in stillness and betray themselves in motion.

## Learning from a director without copying them

The studio uses contemporary African visual direction as study material — specifically, work
that treats cultural objects as speculative artefacts, builds meaning through ritual and
repetition, uses weathering and transformation as texture, and grounds quiet surrealism in
ordinary Black life. Recurring craft notes worth internalising: designed tableaux over
captured moments, deliberate lens choice as a stated decision, music-led editing, sparse
caption copy.

What you take from a director is the *grammar*: how an artefact is framed to read as
significant, how repetition accumulates rather than bores, how restraint creates weight. What
you do not take is their shots, their scripts, their locations or their specific images. You
compose original scenes in the grammar you learned.

This is the same discipline as the UGC teardown, applied to art direction instead of
retention.

## Building the reference board

For each project, assemble a board that fixes:

- The three palette values, as actual swatches, not adjectives.
- Two or three lighting setups you will reuse — direction, quality, contrast ratio.
- A lens and framing convention. Pick a focal character and hold it; mixing wide and
  compressed perspective across a video destroys cohesion faster than any colour error.
- Texture references: fabric, skin, stone, plant, dust.
- A short list of forbidden looks — the defaults you are actively refusing.

That last item does more work than the rest combined. Name the clichés you will not ship:
orange-teal grade, lens flares, slow-motion for its own sake, symmetrical hero shots, glossy
skin.

## Consistency across a long piece

- Freeze the character reference set before shot one, and version it.
- Reuse the same lighting setups rather than inventing per shot.
- Grade to the palette at the end, as one pass over everything, so drift gets corrected
  globally.
- Review the whole set as a contact sheet, not shot by shot. Cohesion is only visible in
  aggregate.

## Deliverable

A reference board with swatches, lighting setups, a lens convention, texture plates, and a
written list of forbidden looks.
