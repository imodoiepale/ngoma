# Safety, rights and the creative brief

Nothing here starts with a prompt. It starts with a brief that answers who is on screen, what
is being claimed, and who owns the material you are working from. Skip this and the rest of
the course still produces images — it just produces images you cannot safely publish.

## The brief

One page, in a file, versioned with the project. Written before you open a workflow.

- **Performer.** Whose face and whose voice? If it is a real person you need written consent
  covering likeness *and* voice, and covering synthetic reproduction specifically. Generic
  photo-release wording usually does not cover it.
- **Product.** Exact name, model, offer, price, and the market it sells in.
- **Audience and market.** This drives language, register and pacing more than any other input.
- **Language.** English, Kenyan English, standard Kiswahili, Sheng, French, or a controlled
  blend. Decided before you write, not after.
- **Claim sheet.** Every factual assertion the ad makes, with a source per claim.
- **Rights.** Where each reference image, video, song and font came from, and what that source
  actually permits.

## Inspiration versus copying

The method is to study a reference ad and reuse its *structure*: shot boundaries, beat timing,
information density, where the hook lands, how hands interact with the product, what the
camera does. Structure and pacing are craft, and craft is learnable.

What you do not reuse is the expression — their script wording, their voice performance, their
shots, their footage, their music. You write new lines and build new scenes that hit the same
rhythm.

The practical test: put your ad beside the reference. Would a viewer recognise a shared
*technique*, or the *same ad*? The first is fine. The second is not, and it is also weaker
marketing, because you are selling a different product to a different audience.

## Reference material in this studio

The research library contains downloaded tutorials, transcripts and creator workflow files
from named third-party channels. Treat all of it as **private study material**. It exists so
technique can be analysed offline and so analysis does not have to be repeated. It is not a
source of footage, scripts, voices or lesson content to ship or resell.

Where this course points you at an external video, it links to the creator's original upload
and credits them. That is the correct way to use it.

## Consent for voice, specifically

Synthetic voice is the sharpest edge in the pipeline. Two rules:

1. **Prefer the real recording.** If your performer can record the script, use that take as
   the timing master. Faster, better-sounding, and it removes the consent question entirely.
2. **Only synthesize a specific person's voice with documented permission for that use.**
   Having a voice note from someone is not permission to clone them.

For a generic voice with no real person behind it, say so in the brief, so nobody later
assumes there was a performer to credit or pay.

## Deliverable

An approved brief, a claim sheet with a source per claim, and a rights checklist naming every
asset and its permission status. Everything downstream references these three files.
