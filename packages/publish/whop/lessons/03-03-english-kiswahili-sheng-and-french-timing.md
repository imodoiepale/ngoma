# English, Kiswahili, Sheng and French timing

Localisation is not translation. A translated script is the right words at the wrong speed,
and wrong speed breaks the edit, the lip sync and the CTA placement all at once.

## Timing comes first

Rule: **localise after timing is locked, and retime every version.**

The same message takes a different number of syllables in every language. Translate a 28-second
English script into Kiswahili and you will typically overrun; force it back into the original
cut and the delivery turns rushed and the last beat gets clipped — usually the CTA, which is
the one beat that pays.

So the workflow is:

1. Write and lock the English master with beat timings.
2. For each language, rewrite to the *beat*, not to the sentence. Each beat keeps its duration
   and its job; the wording is free to change completely.
3. Record or synthesize.
4. Re-measure. Adjust the wording, not the edit, until it fits.

Budget words per beat rather than per script. A 2.5-second beat carries roughly 6–8 English
words at ad pace; write to that ceiling per language and you stop discovering overruns in the
edit.

## Register per variant

**English (international).** Neutral, exportable, safest for spec-heavy lines. Loses local
warmth and reads as an advert.

**Kenyan English.** Same lexicon, local rhythm and stress. Often the best default for a
Kenyan market — credible without excluding anyone.

**Standard Kiswahili.** Broad reach across East Africa, works across age groups, appropriate
for formal or institutional products. Longer than English for the same content; plan the extra
time.

**Sheng.** High trust and high stopping power with urban youth, and actively alienating
outside that group. It is generational and it moves fast, so a phrase that landed last year
can read as try-hard now. Never write it from a wordlist — get it from a current speaker.

**French.** For West and Central African or wider francophone markets. Longer than English
again. Its register conventions differ enough that a direct rendering of an English hook often
lands as either stiff or oddly casual; treat it as a rewrite.

## Code-switching

Blended lines are natural in Nairobi speech and effective in ads, but only when the switch
points are deliberate. Mark them in the script. Useful pattern: switch *into* the local
register for the emotional beats — hook, reaction, CTA — and hold the neutral register for
factual lines like price, spec and delivery terms, where precision matters and slang adds
ambiguity.

Never switch mid-clause for effect. It reads as a machine translation artefact.

## Delivery markup

Keep performance direction **separate from spoken text**, so the spoken text is exactly what
gets read. The project's voice specification covers:

- Speaker age range.
- Regional character.
- Energy, and where it changes.
- Pace per beat.
- Pause lengths, in fractions of a second.
- Code-switch points, marked inline.
- Emotional turns.
- Pronunciation notes for brand names, prices, URLs and the CTA.

Phonetics for those four categories are not optional. A mispronounced brand name or a garbled
price is a wasted spend, and a synthesized voice will confidently mangle both.

## Subtitle safety

Most viewers watch muted, so the subtitle is the ad.

- Keep lines short enough to sit on one or two lines at platform default size — around 32
  characters per line is a safe working limit.
- Never split a price, a phone number or a CTA across a line break.
- Check that the local-language line does not overflow when the caption font falls back for
  accented characters.
- Keep captions clear of the platform's own UI overlay zones at the bottom and right.

## Native review

Every localised script is reviewed by a speaker from the target market before recording. Not a
fluent outsider — someone who lives in the register. They are checking for the thing you
cannot check: whether it sounds like a person or like an advert pretending to be a person.

For Sheng specifically, treat this as mandatory rather than advisory.

## Deliverable

One locked English master with beat timings, plus Kiswahili, Sheng and French variants, each
retimed to the same beat map, each with a word budget per beat, delivery markup, phonetic
notes, and a named native reviewer.
